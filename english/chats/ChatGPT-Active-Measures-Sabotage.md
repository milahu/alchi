Friedrich describes essentially the exact nightmare you're getting at:

> “The regime’s total control of all the means of mass communication, as well as post, telephone, and telegraph…”

He then argues that this, combined with secret-police surveillance and the state's monopoly on weapons, makes organizing effective opposition **“well-nigh hopeless.”** ([rexresearch1.com](https://www.rexresearch1.com/TotalitarianismLibrary/TotalitarianDictatorshipAutocracyFriedrich.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That's probably the single closest historical formulation to your thought. What's especially interesting is that Friedrich doesn't merely say *“the government censors opposition.”* His argument is more structural:

**No communication → no coordination → no organization → no effective opposition.**

And notice that he explicitly includes **post** and **telephone/telegraph**-so your point about physical mail isn't an anachronistic concern. Control over the mundane channels by which people can find one another is central to the problem.

### 2. Hannah Arendt - *The Origins of Totalitarianism*

Arendt gets at an even deeper version of your idea: **the regime doesn't merely control what people say; it destroys the possibility of people acting together.**

The Library of Congress's presentation of Arendt emphasizes the relationship between political isolation, communication, and the ability to act collectively. In her analysis, tyranny produces a kind of political impotence because people cannot establish the relationships necessary for collective action. A particularly useful concept here is **isolation**.

Arendt's insight can almost be reformulated as:

> **A person can oppose a government privately. Political resistance requires people to discover one another and act together.**

That distinction is extremely important. A government doesn't necessarily have to convince everyone that it is legitimate. It may only need to make everyone **believe that everyone else accepts it**.

That's a much darker mechanism.

### 3. The “plurality” problem

Arendt's thought also points toward something slightly different from censorship:

**Political power requires plurality.**

If I think *“I hate this system”* but cannot communicate with you, meet you, trust you, or even know whether you exist, my opposition remains psychologically and politically isolated.

So the state doesn't necessarily have to suppress **every individual thought**. It can suppress the **network connecting individual thoughts**.

You could formulate the distinction like this:

> **Censorship controls what people can say. Total political isolation controls whether people can discover that they agree.**

That's very close to the phenomenon you're describing.

A useful way of putting the Havelian problem is:

Yes. That expands the conceptual frame considerably. The important distinction is between **intelligence as observation** and **intelligence/security services as intervention**.

“Active measures” is a particularly useful historical term because it breaks the popular mental model of the secret service as essentially *a camera pointed at society*. In reality, intelligence organizations have historically had functions intended to **alter the information environment, influence actors, disrupt organizations, deceive adversaries, and shape perceptions**. The Soviet concept of *aktivnye meropriyatiya* is the classic example, but analogous categories exist in many states.

There is also a second-order problem in what you're describing:

> **An organization whose activities depend on secrecy has an inherent incentive to influence how the public understands the organization's activities.**

That doesn't mean every public statement is necessarily deceptive, nor that every intelligence service secretly controls public discourse. But analytically, **public perception of an intelligence service can itself become part of the information environment in which the service operates.**

### A useful conceptual ladder

I'd separate the phenomenon into roughly five layers:

**1. Passive intelligence**

> *“What is happening?”*

Surveillance, interception, reconnaissance, informants, intelligence analysis, etc.

**2. Operational intervention**

> *“How can we change what is happening?”*

Disruption, infiltration, arrests through the relevant authorities, interference with adversary organizations, covert operations, etc.

**3. Information operations / active measures**

> *“How can we change what people believe, know, communicate, or do?”*

Propaganda, disinformation, influence operations, covert media activity, manipulation of narratives, etc.

**4. Structural control**

> *“How can we change the channels through which people interact?”*

This is particularly close to your original idea: communications infrastructure, information platforms, censorship mechanisms, financial/organizational choke points, travel, association, etc.

**5. Meta-information**

> *“How can we influence people's understanding of the preceding four layers?”*

This is where your **meta-censorship / perception-management** idea becomes interesting.

The public debate isn't merely about *what the state does*. It is also about **what the public believes the state does**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### The really interesting distinction: censorship vs. suppression of coordination

I'd actually move away from “online censorship” as the central concept.

The deeper phenomenon is:

> **Control over the possibility of coordination.**

Censorship is one instrument.

Suppose 10,000 people independently oppose something.

The state could:

- prevent them from communicating;
- prevent them from identifying one another;
- make communication unreliable;
- infiltrate their communications;
- make participants fear that communications are monitored;
- disrupt meetings;
- interfere with organizations;
- financially isolate organizers;
- discredit potential leaders;
- flood the information environment with competing narratives;
- create uncertainty about who is genuine;
- or simply make everyone believe that *nobody else is willing to act*.

Notice that some of those don't require censorship at all.

**Surveillance can produce self-censorship.  
Disinformation can produce distrust.  
Infiltration can produce paranoia.  
Isolation can prevent organization.  
Propaganda can produce perceived consensus.**

And these mechanisms can reinforce one another.

## This connects directly back to your original question

Your original:

> “How do you organize a resistance if your enemy controls all your communication channels and all your supply lines?”

could therefore become a more general theoretical question:

> **How can a decentralized opposition emerge when a centralized power controls the infrastructure required for opposition to become coordinated action?**

That's a much broader question than “Can the government censor the internet?”

It's a question about **power asymmetry and organizational emergence**.

And there is a fascinating paradox:

> **The more centralized the infrastructure of society becomes, the easier it may become to control the coordination layer on which decentralized opposition depends.**

That's one reason your “over-centralization” intuition is more interesting than simply saying *“governments censor people.”*

## “Active measures” also complicates the passive/active distinction

There's an important historical terminology issue here.

The Soviet term **aktivnye meropriyatiya** (“active measures”) specifically referred to covert political influence activities, including things such as propaganda, disinformation, political influence, and related operations. Western intelligence agencies have also had operational/covert-action functions, although their terminology and legal structures differ.

So I would **not** use “active measures” as a generic synonym for *everything a secret service does beyond intelligence gathering*. That would blur some important distinctions.

Instead, you could use it as the historical gateway into a larger taxonomy:

> **intelligence collection → covert action → active measures → information operations → influence operations → perception management**

The exact boundaries differ by country and period, but the conceptual progression is useful.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## And your “secret” paradox is genuinely worth investigating

I'd phrase the hypothesis carefully:

> **Secret organizations have a structural interest in maintaining secrecy about their capabilities and activities, and therefore public representations of those organizations are themselves an object of strategic interest.**

That is stronger—and more defensible—than:

> “Secret services deliberately deceive the public about everything they do.”

The former is an institutional hypothesis. The latter is an empirical claim that would require evidence for each particular case.

It also gives you a fascinating **recursive structure**:

**Intelligence service**

↓ observes

**society**

↓ intervenes in

**society's information environment**

↓ which determines

**what society believes about the intelligence service**

↓ which affects

**how society responds to the intelligence service**

So the organization isn't merely operating *inside* the information environment.

**It can potentially operate on the information environment in which its own behavior is interpreted.**

That's the “meta” level you're getting at.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### There is an even darker formulation

The ultimate version of your thesis isn't:

> **“The secret service can censor resistance.”**

It's:

> **“A sufficiently powerful state can make the organizational preconditions of resistance themselves objects of state power.”**

That includes **communication, association, trust, mobility, financing, information, leadership, and collective perception**.

And once you frame it that way, your original problem becomes almost a theorem-like question:

> **If political resistance requires coordination, and the state possesses overwhelming control over the infrastructure of coordination, what mechanism remains by which resistance can scale from individual dissent into collective action?**

That, I think, is the much richer research question you're circling around.
