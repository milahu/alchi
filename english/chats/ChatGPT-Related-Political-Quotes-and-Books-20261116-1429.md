The two passages you provided connect to a long tradition of **political pessimism, elite theory, anti-democratic philosophy, and authoritarian realism**.

The second passage is particularly interesting because it combines several distinct arguments:

1.  **Democratic skepticism:** Ordinary voters are poorly informed, irrational, or easily manipulated.
2.  **Elite theory:** Every society is ultimately governed by a small, powerful minority, regardless of its formal constitution.
3.  **Democracy as theater:** Elections provide an appearance of popular sovereignty while real decisions are made elsewhere.
4.  **Authoritarian realism:** If elite domination is inevitable, openly acknowledging it might be more honest than maintaining democratic institutions.
5.  **Technocratic or military authoritarianism:** Concentrated power is presented as more efficient than democratic deliberation.

These ideas appear in works by Plato, Machiavelli, Gaetano Mosca, Vilfredo Pareto, Robert Michels, Joseph Schumpeter, C. Wright Mills, and contemporary critics of democracy.

One important distinction: **arguing that elites dominate democracies does not necessarily imply that dictatorship would be more honest, efficient, or desirable.** Some thinkers use elite theory to justify concentrated authority; others use it to argue for stronger democratic accountability.

## 4. A particularly relevant passage from James Burnham

In *The Machiavellians*, Burnham develops an argument very close to your description: that democracy understood as literal self-government is an ideal that does not describe actual political organization. He treats democratic institutions as one mechanism through which ruling minorities govern.

But he draws an important distinction between the **myth of popular self-government** and the **real possibility of political liberty**, arguing that opposition and competing power centers can constrain elites even when direct rule by the majority is impossible. <Cite ref="turn689728search5"/>

That distinction is useful for examining the logical jump in your second quotation: from *elites always exist* to *therefore unrestricted elite dictatorship should be established*.

## 5. Terms for finding more material

These concepts will lead you to further quotations, books, and essays:

| Concept | Central idea |
|----|----|
| **Elite theory** | A minority holds decisive political power |
| **Iron law of oligarchy** | Organizations tend toward rule by a few |
| **Democratic elitism** | Elections select competing elites rather than eliminate elite rule |
| **Political realism** | Political analysis prioritizes power and institutions over ideals |
| **Epistocracy** | Political authority should favor the knowledgeable |
| **Neoreaction / Dark Enlightenment** | Radical criticism of democracy and arguments for alternative hierarchical regimes |
| **Authoritarian technocracy** | Concentrated authority justified through expertise and administrative efficiency |

The especially useful distinction is between **descriptive elite theory** (who actually rules?) and **normative authoritarianism** (who ought to rule, and with what limits?).

If you're searching for the intellectual origins of the second passage, the combination of **Michels, Mosca, Burnham, and Yarvin** provides a direct route from classical elite theory to contemporary arguments about making concentrated political authority explicit.
