Yes — **“controlled opposition”** captures one part of what you mean, but it’s probably **not the best term for the whole phenomenon**.

What you’re describing has several distinct mechanisms:

1.  **A legitimate/radical demand is accepted rhetorically.**
2.  It is **institutionalized and reduced to a safe, minimal version**.
3.  The implementation becomes **bureaucratic, performative, or tokenistic**.
4.  People are then told the problem has been addressed.
5.  The watered-down version can actually **discredit the original ideal**, because people experience the institution's version and become cynical about the ideal itself.
6.  More radical critics are **delegitimized**, while moderate critics are satisfied by symbolic concessions.

### The shortest phrases I'd consider

**“Co-optation and dilution.”**

This is probably the most academically recognizable pair. A system **co-opts** an oppositional idea by adopting it, then **dilutes** it until it no longer threatens the system.

> *The system neutralizes criticism through co-optation and dilution.*

Or:

**“Institutionalized tokenism.”**

This emphasizes the phenomenon where something that ought to be substantive becomes a **token gesture** or box-ticking exercise.

> *Teamwork becomes institutionalized tokenism: the school technically teaches it, while structurally undermining it.*

Another very good phrase:

**“Performative compliance.”**

The institution performs the appearance of satisfying a demand without substantially changing the underlying system.

> *The school doesn't actually cultivate responsibility; it performs compliance with the ideal of responsibility.*

### But I think your *specific* phenomenon has an even sharper formulation

**“The domestication of ideals.”**

That's a strong phrase for what you're describing.

An ideal that could potentially challenge the institution gets **domesticated**: incorporated into the institution in a harmless form.

For example:

> **“The system domesticates the ideals it cannot openly reject.”**

“Teach critical thinking” becomes memorizing the officially approved definition of critical thinking.  
“Teach responsibility” becomes obeying deadlines and rules.  
“Teach teamwork” becomes completing assigned group projects.  
“Teach literacy” becomes passing standardized literacy requirements.

The **word survives while the substance changes**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### If you want to emphasize the *backfire* effect

Your intuition about “good intentions are worse than no intentions” is interesting, but I'd phrase it as:

**“Perverse institutionalization.”**

or

**“The corruption of ideals through institutionalization.”**

The key idea is that once an ideal is converted into an institutional requirement, the institution's incentives can transform it into something almost opposite to the original intention.

A related concept is the **cobra effect**: an intervention intended to solve a problem produces incentives that make the problem worse.

But your phenomenon is somewhat different because the problem isn't merely unintended consequences. **The institution may satisfy the literal demand while evading its substantive meaning.**

That's closer to:

**“Goodhart's law.”**

> *When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.*

If “literacy” becomes a measurable school outcome, the institution can optimize for **literacy scores** rather than actual intellectual competence. If “teamwork” becomes a curriculum objective, students can learn how to **complete the teamwork assignment** rather than how to cooperate meaningfully.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### And there's a particularly useful term for the “minimum viable version”

**“Box-ticking.”**

or more formally:

**“Tick-box compliance.”**

This is probably the clearest everyday description of the mechanism:

> **The institution converts substantive ideals into tick-box requirements.**

And that gives you a very compact formulation of your whole argument:

> **“The institutionalization of ideals turns them into tick-box compliance.”**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Your “controlled opposition” idea

I wouldn't use **controlled opposition** unless you specifically want to make the stronger claim that the opposition is **deliberately controlled by the regime**.

There is a much broader and more defensible concept:

**“Co-optation.”**

The system doesn't necessarily have to secretly manufacture its critics. It can simply **absorb their demands**, translate them into institutional language, and thereby neutralize them.

There's also:

**“Repressive tolerance”** — Herbert Marcuse's term for the idea that a system can tolerate forms of dissent in ways that ultimately help preserve the existing order.

And:

**“Recuperation”** — especially in radical/critical theory, meaning that potentially subversive ideas are absorbed by the mainstream and turned into harmless commodities, styles, slogans, or institutional practices.

So if you're deliberately writing in a radical/critical-theory vocabulary, **co-optation**, **recuperation**, and **repressive tolerance** are much closer to your idea than “controlled opposition.”
