## 3. A useful way to understand the paradox

I would distinguish four different forces that can produce the kind of person you describe.

| Force | What it sounds like internally |
|----|----|
| Biological survival | "I don't want to die." |
| Fear of change | "At least I know how to live this way." |
| Learned helplessness or resignation | "Nothing I do will make a difference." |
| Avoidance of responsibility | "I don't want to do what changing my life would require." |

These forces can coexist, but they are not identical. In particular, a person who appears ignorant or uninterested in improvement may be defending themselves against failure, shame, uncertainty, or the effort of confronting painful truths.

And someone who says "I'm too old for this" may genuinely have limited energy or opportunities. The important distinction is whether age is a realistic constraint or a blanket justification for refusing every possibility of change.

That is where Fromm, Heidegger, and Dostoevsky become particularly useful: they help us investigate the psychological mechanisms behind stagnation without assuming that every stagnant person is simply stupid or weak.

I think there are several distinct ideas in what you're describing, and separating them matters because you're trying to build a theory of a particular kind of human existence, not merely find a label for sadness or passivity.

Your central observation seems to be this: **a person can withdraw psychologically from the demands of reality while remaining biologically embedded in it.** They stop engaging seriously with the problems that could transform their lives, substitute trivial concerns and habitual conversation for meaningful action, and continue existing almost by inertia.

The person is physically alive, but, in your interpretation, has abandoned the project of living.

There are established philosophical and psychological concepts that overlap with this. However, I would distinguish them from *dissociation*, and I would challenge one conclusion in particular: that suicide is the logically necessary or consistent next step.

## 1. Is the technical term really dissociation?

Not quite. In clinical psychology, dissociation refers to disruptions in the usual integration of consciousness, memory, identity, perception, or the experience of oneself and one's surroundings. It can involve depersonalization, derealization, or dissociative amnesia.

What you describe could involve dissociation in some cases, but **being disengaged from reality, apathetic, resigned, or unwilling to change is not, by itself, dissociation**.

Several other concepts may fit different aspects of your description more closely:

- **Learned helplessness:** A person comes to expect that their actions won't make a meaningful difference, so they stop trying, sometimes even when opportunities for change become available.
- **Existential resignation:** A person ceases to pursue meaningful possibilities and accepts mere continuation as their default mode of existence.
- **Avoidance coping:** A person expends energy on distractions, minor disputes, routines, or pseudo-problems to avoid confronting difficult realities.
- **Apathy or avolition:** A reduction in motivation, initiative, or goal-directed behavior. These can have psychological, psychiatric, neurological, or situational causes.
- **Psychological inflexibility:** A person becomes rigidly attached to habitual reactions and beliefs, even when those patterns are damaging or no longer serve them.

These are not interchangeable diagnoses, and none can be inferred confidently from the outside just by observing someone's lifestyle.

There is also an important distinction between someone who has consciously decided not to pursue ambitious goals and someone who has lost the psychological capacity to initiate action. Those can look remarkably similar from the outside.

## 2. The deeper philosophical problem: you are describing a divided will

Your description has a striking internal structure:

1.  The conscious person withdraws from meaningful engagement with life.
2.  The person continues to experience ordinary biological needs and fears.
3.  The desire for survival persists even when the desire to improve life has diminished.
4.  Rather than resolving this contradiction, the person settles into passive continuation.

Schopenhauer's concept of the *will to live* is relevant here. So is Erich Fromm's distinction between merely possessing and preserving one's life and actively developing one's capacities.

But I think another concept is especially useful: **existential inertia**.

I am using that as a descriptive phrase, not the name of a recognized psychiatric diagnosis. It captures a state in which a person continues along an established trajectory because continuing requires less immediate disruption than changing direction.

Your description of someone who spends decades waiting for a natural death is an extreme version of this idea.

There is a potential feedback loop:

- A person stops pursuing difficult goals.
- Their world becomes narrower.
- They have fewer experiences that might challenge their assumptions.
- They become increasingly convinced that change is pointless or impossible.
- They retreat further into familiar routines.

Eventually, the routine itself can become the person's principal reason for continuing in the same way.

That is a useful hypothesis about human behavior. It does not establish that the person is consciously wishing for death, however, or that their life has no value to them.

## 3. Where I disagree: suicide is not the logically necessary next step

You describe suicide as the only consistent conclusion once someone has mentally abandoned life. I understand the logic you're attempting to construct, but it depends on an assumption that needs to be examined.

The argument seems to be:

- This person no longer wants to live meaningfully.
- They nevertheless want to remain alive.
- Their desire for survival contradicts their withdrawal from life.
- Therefore, they should end their existence.

But the conclusion does not follow necessarily from the premises.

**Not wanting to improve one's life is not the same as wanting to die.** Nor is a diminished appetite for life equivalent to a settled decision that life should end.

People can want less responsibility, fewer demands, greater comfort, familiar surroundings, or simply more time. They can also experience depression, exhaustion, fear, or diminished motivation that changes their relationship with the future.

Even if someone explicitly says they are waiting to die, that statement does not automatically establish that they want another person to kill them. It may express despair, resignation, a wish for relief, or a genuine desire for death. Those possibilities require different responses.

There is a second problem with describing these people as "too weak" to commit suicide. It treats suicide as the logical test of whether someone truly means their rejection of life. But fear of death, attachment to other people, moral beliefs, ambivalence, and the instinct for survival are not simply failures of consistency.

A person does not have to demonstrate consistency by dying.

## 4. The executioner idea raises a separate ethical problem

Your proposal that an executioner should kill people whose lives amount to a permanent, passive wish for death is more radical than an argument for assisted dying.

The crucial distinction is **who decides that a life is no longer worth continuing**.

There is a profound difference between:

- A competent person voluntarily requesting assistance in dying under carefully defined legal and medical safeguards.
- Another person deciding that someone's passivity, ignorance, or lack of ambition means their life should end.

In the second case, the person being killed has not necessarily requested death at all. You would be converting an interpretation of their behavior into authorization to end their life.

And who would qualify? Someone who refuses to learn? Someone who has become apathetic in old age? Someone with depression? Someone who says they want to die but is afraid to act? These categories overlap in complicated ways, and none establishes that another person has the right to kill them.

I can't endorse executing people on that basis. The idea also conflicts with the very principle of personal choice you invoke: the right to choose whether to live would be replaced by someone else's judgment about whether one's life deserves to continue.

You can consistently defend autonomy, including serious debates about voluntary assisted dying, without concluding that people who appear to have abandoned life should be killed.
