## One important distinction

I suspect what you're looking for is actually quite rare in fiction. Most vigilante stories need an exceptionally evil antagonist to justify the protagonist's violence to the audience. They focus on murder, rape, organized crime, or corruption on a massive scale.

*Mr Inbetween* is unusual because it can take an ordinary social interaction and make it the basis of a moral confrontation. Ray doesn't always need to discover a criminal conspiracy. Sometimes he encounters someone behaving badly, decides that the behavior is unacceptable, and acts on that judgment. The everyday setting is part of what makes his response interesting. \<Cite refs={\["turn716307search4","turn716307search29"\]}/\>

Stories that explore that ambiguity are likely to be more satisfying for you than straightforward superhero-style vigilantism.

These two ideas point toward a much more interesting niche than ordinary vigilante fiction. I'd describe the common theme as **informal moral authority**: people who take responsibility for enforcing the rules that make ordinary social life possible, even when the official rules, institutions, or other people's indifference prevent them from doing so.

Your two examples explore different versions of the same idea.

## 1. The good cop who crosses the line for the right reasons

The protagonist has legitimate authority, but uses it in ways that go beyond - or sometimes directly against - the official rules. The dramatic question isn't simply whether the cop is corrupt. It's whether the protagonist can use power to protect people without becoming an abuser of that power himself.

This includes police officers, nightclub bouncers, security guards, and other people who are in a position to intervene when someone is intimidating or exploiting others.

## 2. The neighborhood that refuses to tolerate predatory behavior

Here, the protagonist isn't necessarily a police officer or professional enforcer. Ordinary people recognize a recurring pattern of antisocial behavior and collectively decide to stop being easy targets.

The key is that the group isn't responding to one extraordinary atrocity. It's responding to the cumulative damage caused by people who exploit the fact that individual victims are reluctant to confront them.

That creates a particularly interesting tension: **how do you preserve a community's trust without allowing the people who abuse that trust to thrive?** And how do you prevent a group that begins with good intentions from becoming a gang that punishes people merely for being unpopular?

## A final thought about why this kind of story is so rare

I think you've identified a genuine gap in mainstream fiction. The conventional vigilante story usually needs a dramatic trigger: a murdered family member, a horrific assault, or a criminal organization. The protagonist's violence is then justified by the exceptional severity of the original offense.

Your interest is almost the inverse. You're interested in what happens **before wrongdoing becomes spectacular enough to attract everyone's attention**.

A person repeatedly cheats acquaintances, intimidates a neighbor, breaks promises, or takes advantage of someone's reluctance to cause a scene. Every individual incident is easy to dismiss. But the repeated pattern changes how everyone behaves: people become guarded, generosity becomes risky, and strangers are treated as potential adversaries.

That theme has several useful search terms if you want to discover more obscure works:

- **Everyday vigilantism** - informal enforcement in ordinary social life.
- **Quality-of-life crime** - relatively low-level conduct that can affect shared spaces and community wellbeing.
- **Social trust and antisocial behavior** - stories about how small violations of trust alter a whole community.
- **Informal social control** - how people in a community maintain shared standards without relying exclusively on police or courts.
- **The wandering enforcer** - a protagonist who repeatedly encounters new local injustices rather than pursuing a single nemesis.
