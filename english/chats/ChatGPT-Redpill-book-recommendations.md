Yes - and I think you've identified a real gap in the usual “redpill” canon.

A lot of the books you listed are essentially **diagnostic**:

> *Here is why the system is broken. Here is how you were manipulated. Here is why institutions can't be trusted.*

But then they stop.

What you're looking for is more like:

> **“Fine. Assume the diagnosis is correct. Now how do 20, 50, 500 or 5,000 people actually build something better?”**

That leads into a somewhat different intellectual tradition: **communalism, localism, mutual aid, self-governance, intentional communities, cooperative economics, decentralization, secession/local autonomy, polycentric governance, and practical organizing.**

And there are some *very* good books here.

## 🔥 The strongest matches

### 1. Murray Bookchin - *The Next Revolution*

Probably the **single best match** to what you're describing.

Bookchin's answer isn't “withdraw from society and live off-grid.” It's essentially:

**build autonomous local political institutions → organize communities → create participatory assemblies → federate them.**

His concept of **libertarian municipalism/communalism** is remarkably close to your “organizing community + decentralization + flat-ish hierarchy” description. He explicitly argues for active citizens rather than passive consumers, with local assemblies confederating upward. ([The Anarchist Library](https://www.theanarchistlibrary.org/library/murray-bookchin-next-revolution?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

If *Endgame* is:

> Civilization is fundamentally destructive.

Bookchin asks:

> **Okay. What political structure replaces it?**

I'd start here.

Also consider:

- **Murray Bookchin - *The Ecology of Freedom***
- **Murray Bookchin - *Post-Scarcity Anarchism***
- **Murray Bookchin - *Remaking Society***
- **Murray Bookchin - *Social Ecology and Communalism***

*Post-Scarcity Anarchism* is especially interesting because it explicitly argues that modern technological capacity can make decentralized self-administration possible rather than requiring centralized bureaucratic control. ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Post_scarcity_Anarchism.html?id=ZPlEAAAAYAAJ&utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 2. Elinor Ostrom - *Governing the Commons*

This is probably the **most important book if you want evidence rather than ideology**.

Ostrom studied communities that actually managed common resources without either:

- centralized state control, or
- pure private-property/market arrangements.

She identified recurring institutional principles that allowed communities to govern themselves successfully.

That's basically your question in empirical form:

> **Can ordinary people actually organize themselves without a giant hierarchy?**

Answer: **yes, under particular conditions.**

And Ostrom is especially valuable because she doesn't romanticize decentralization. She investigates **what actually works and what fails**.

If you like this direction, also look at **Derek Wall's *Elinor Ostrom's Rules for Radicals***, which specifically interprets Ostrom's work as a basis for cooperative alternatives beyond conventional markets and states. ([JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1vz4931?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 3. James C. Scott - *The Art of Not Being Governed*

You already have Scott's *Seeing Like a State* on your list.

This is the one I'd add immediately.

It asks something much more interesting than “states are bad”:

> **How have real populations historically organized themselves to avoid incorporation into states?**

Scott studies the peoples of the highlands of Southeast Asia who maintained relatively autonomous forms of social organization and resisted state incorporation.

It's basically **anti-state anthropology** rather than political philosophy.

And then there's:

### James C. Scott - *Against the Grain*

This goes further back and asks how early states emerged, why people became sedentary, taxation, grain agriculture, forced labor, population control, etc.

Together:

**Against the Grain → The Art of Not Being Governed**

is an excellent historical foundation for the “small-scale autonomous society” idea.

# 🧬 The “tribalism / small-scale society” branch

This is another rabbit hole I'd recommend to you.

### 8. Joseph Henrich - *The WEIRDest People in the World*

Not “redpill” in tone at all.

But extremely interesting for your question.

Henrich examines how Western psychology and institutions became unusually individualistic and explores the historical development of kinship, marriage, religion and social organization.

It gives you a much better empirical basis for asking:

> **Are humans actually adapted to huge anonymous societies?**

rather than simply assuming that they aren't.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 9. Christopher Boehm - *Hierarchy in the Forest*

**Very relevant to your “flat hierarchy / tribalism” interest.**

Boehm examines egalitarian hunter-gatherer societies and the mechanisms they used to prevent individuals from becoming dominant.

The interesting concept is essentially:

> **Humans aren't necessarily naturally hierarchical; small groups can actively suppress would-be alpha individuals.**

That leads to fascinating questions about gossip, ostracism, coalition formation, ridicule, consensus, leadership, etc.

In other words:

**How do you prevent the guy who starts the organization from eventually becoming the guy who owns the organization?**

That's a *very* important problem for decentralized movements.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 10. David Graeber & David Wengrow - *The Dawn of Everything*

I'd strongly recommend this one for you.

Its central historical argument is essentially that humans have experimented with **far more forms of social organization** than the conventional “tribe → chiefdom → state → civilization” story suggests.

They examine societies that deliberately shifted between different forms of hierarchy and egalitarianism.

It is much more nuanced than “tribalism good, state bad.”

And that's precisely why it's useful.

This is perhaps the **most practically interesting branch** of all.

Instead of:

> overthrow the system

the strategy becomes:

> **build things people can use instead of the system.**

Think:

- mutual-aid networks
- cooperatives
- community currencies
- local food production
- community land trusts
- homeschooling networks
- independent schools
- local businesses
- maker spaces
- decentralized communication
- community defense
- local energy
- credit unions
- intentional communities
- parallel institutions

Some books worth exploring:

### 11. John McKnight & Peter Block - *The Abundant Community*

How communities can rebuild capacity by relying on **local skills, relationships and associations** rather than professionalized institutions.

### 12. John McKnight & John Kretzmann - *Building Communities from the Inside Out*

Very practical **asset-based community development**: identify what people in a community already have and organize around those capabilities.

### 13. Peter Gelderloos - *Anarchy Works*

This one is particularly interesting because it attempts to answer:

> **“But who would actually provide X without a state?”**

It presents examples of decentralized organization dealing with food, healthcare, conflict resolution, security, etc.

You don't have to agree with the politics to find the *case studies* useful.

That fits your idea of:

> **children having their own society rather than merely being members of adult society-in-training.**

And this matters for your “children should lead” idea.
